# Active Writing: Against AI Passivity

## The Problem

AI-generated stories are passive. They hedge. They balance. They refuse to commit. This comes from how LLMs are trained—to be neutral, to avoid offense, to present "both sides."

This makes for terrible fiction.

Good stories are opinionated. They take positions. They make the audience feel something strong—love, hate, discomfort, recognition, outrage, hope. A story that offends no one moves no one.

## Symptoms of Passive Writing

### 1. Hedged Character Motivations

**Passive:**
> "The antagonist, while sometimes harsh, had reasons for his behavior that stemmed from his own difficult past."

**Active:**
> "The antagonist believes he is saving his people. He is wrong, but he will burn villages to prove it."

The passive version excuses. The active version lets the character be wrong without apologizing for him.

### 2. Balanced Villainy

**Passive:**
> "Both sides had valid points. The empire provided stability, while the rebels sought freedom. Neither was entirely right or wrong."

**Active:**
> "The empire calls it stability. The Kaelish call it slow death. The rebels will kill to stop dying."

The passive version refuses to take a stance. The active version has a point of view—you feel the Kaelish perspective without being told it's "the right one."

### 3. Soft Consequences

**Passive:**
> "The decision was difficult, and there would be challenges ahead, but Mira felt hopeful about the future."

**Active:**
> "Mira voted yes. By spring, three hundred Kaelish would be dead. She would bury her nephew among them. She would not regret the vote."

The passive version gestures at difficulty. The active version shows it, and commits to a position—she doesn't regret it.

### 4. Universal Empathy

**Passive:**
> "Everyone in the story was doing their best given their circumstances. The governor truly believed integration was best for everyone."

**Active:**
> "The governor truly believed integration was best for everyone. This made him more dangerous than a tyrant—you cannot negotiate with a man who thinks he's saving you."

The passive version presents the governor's belief as exculpatory. The active version presents it as the problem.

### 5. Resolved Tensions

**Passive:**
> "In the end, both the empire and the Kaelish found a way to understand each other better, paving the way for a more peaceful future."

**Active:**
> "In the end, the Kaelish were free. The empire called them traitors. History would decide who was right. The Kaelish stopped waiting for history."

The passive version resolves tension through mutual understanding. The active version lets the tension remain—freedom didn't require the empire's approval.

## The Root Cause

LLMs are trained on human feedback that punishes:
- Taking controversial positions
- Making readers uncomfortable
- Presenting "unbalanced" views
- Having characters do bad things without condemnation

This training is appropriate for an assistant answering questions. It is poison for fiction.

## The Fix: Principles for Active Writing

### 1. Characters Have Positions

Every character believes something. They don't "see both sides." They see their side, and they're willing to act on it.

- The protagonist believes staying is death
- The antagonist believes resistance is childish
- The ally believes freedom is worth dying for
- The doubter believes survival matters more than dignity

None of them are "balanced." All of them are human.

### 2. The Story Has a Point of View

The story doesn't need to state a thesis, but it has one. The structure, the emphasis, the ending—all of these encode a position.

A story about leaving an abusive relationship doesn't need to say "abuse is bad." But it does need to commit to a perspective on what makes leaving hard, what makes it possible, and what it costs.

This perspective will make some people uncomfortable. Good.

### 3. Consequences Are Specific and Unhedged

Don't say "there were difficulties." Say what happened.

- Three hundred dead
- Her nephew among them
- Trade routes closed for six years
- Children who never learned their grandparents' language

Specific consequences are harder to hedge. "Challenges ahead" means nothing. "She buried her nephew" means something.

### 4. Let Characters Be Wrong Without Excusing Them

The antagonist can have a backstory. The antagonist can have reasons. But the story doesn't need to present these reasons as exculpatory.

"He was abused as a child" can be context without being excuse. Show it, don't use it to soften judgment.

### 5. Don't Resolve What Shouldn't Be Resolved

Not every conflict ends in mutual understanding. Not every villain is redeemed. Not every loss is compensated by growth.

Some endings are:
- We won, but it cost more than we imagined
- We lost, and we'd do it again
- They never understood, and we stopped needing them to
- It was worth it (even though it shouldn't have been necessary)

These are active endings. They commit to something.

### 6. Opinions Emerge Through Structure, Not Statements

The story's position comes from:
- What it shows vs. what it tells
- Who gets point-of-view access
- What consequences follow what actions
- How the ending relates to the beginning
- What changes and what doesn't

If you show the empire's "stability" as surveillance and cultural erasure, you've taken a position. You don't need a character to say "the empire is bad."

## For This Engine

When building the metaphor tree:

### At Abstract Level
- The protagonist's position should be clear (not "conflicted about both options")
- The antagonist should believe something specific (not "have complex motivations")
- The trap should be real, not "a matter of perspective"

### At Concrete Level
- Name what's at stake specifically
- Don't soften the antagonist's actions with excessive backstory
- Let the concrete world have a point of view (oppression is oppression, not "cultural tension")

### At Structure Level
- Consequences should be specific: who dies, what's lost, what changes
- Don't manufacture mutual understanding at the end
- The transformation should commit to something (not "she learned to see both sides")

### At Render Level
- Dialogue should express positions, not hedge them
- Description should have a point of view (whose eyes are we seeing through?)
- The ending should land somewhere, not hover above all positions

## The Test

After generating any element, ask:

1. **Would anyone disagree with this?** If not, it's probably too passive.
2. **Does this commit to something?** If it could be read as supporting any position, it's hedging.
3. **Would this make someone uncomfortable?** Discomfort isn't the goal, but its absence is a warning sign.
4. **Can I state what this believes?** If the position is unclear, clarify it.

## Remember

The goal is not to be controversial for its own sake. The goal is to write stories that move people. Stories that move people have spines. They stand for something. They don't apologize for existing.

A story about leaving oppression should make you feel the weight of oppression and the cost of freedom—not present a balanced analysis of whether leaving was the right call.

The audience will decide what they think. The story decides what it thinks.
