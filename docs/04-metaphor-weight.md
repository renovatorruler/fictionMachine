# Metaphor Weight: Balance and Distribution

## The Core Principle

Every element is a metaphor, but not every metaphor carries the same weight. A story needs balance—not in the sense of "equal parts happy and sad" but in **density and distribution**. Too much happening at once overwhelms. Too little happening bores. The goal is coherent flow.

## Two Anti-Patterns

### 1. Density Overload

> "She takes a train. On the train, there's a group of activists protesting immigration deportation. One of the activists turns out to be a serial killer who is also her husband's best friend and also a passionate women's rights advocate."

The problem isn't that any single element is wrong. The problem is:
- Too many significant things collide in one scene
- Contrived coincidences (serial killer + husband's friend + activist)
- Every element is "meaningful" so nothing stands out
- The audience can't process this density

### 2. Distributed Trauma Stacking

> "Maria is a Chinese immigrant who was abused as a child, uses a wheelchair, has a best friend with an intellectual disability, works three jobs, and is the sole caretaker for her dying mother."

This looks like depth but isn't. Each element is disconnected from the others. They don't compound meaningfully—they just pile up. The character becomes a collection of conditions rather than a person.

## What Balance Actually Looks Like

A character CAN have multiple difficult circumstances:
- Abusive father in childhood
- Currently in an abusive marriage
- Faces workplace discrimination
- Immigration officer is subtly racist

This works because:
- These elements can connect thematically (patterns of being controlled/dismissed)
- They're distributed across different domains (past, present relationship, work, system)
- They don't all collide in the same scene
- Some are heavier (the marriages), some are lighter (the immigration officer)

## The Spectrum of Weight

### Heavy Metaphors
- Central to the core movement
- Recur throughout the story
- Directly embody the transformation
- Example: Walter White's transformation from teacher to drug lord

### Medium Metaphors
- Support and texture the theme
- Appear at key moments
- Add dimension without dominating
- Example: The Pontiac Aztek (emasculation, mediocrity, settling)

### Light Metaphors
- Glancing thematic touches
- Single scenes or moments
- Advance without announcing
- Example: Kids mocking Walt at the car wash

## Distribution Principles

### Across Time
Don't front-load backstory. Don't save everything for the climax. Distribute weight across the story's timeline.

### Across Space
Don't have everything important happen in one location. Let different spaces carry different metaphorical work.

### Across Characters
The protagonist doesn't carry all the weight. Supporting characters, antagonists, even background figures do metaphorical work.

### Across Scenes
Not every scene is a "big moment." Light scenes make heavy scenes land. Heavy scenes give light scenes meaning.

## The Coincidence Problem

Coincidences in stories feel false when they're too convenient or too loaded:

**Bad:** "The detective investigating her husband's murder turns out to be her estranged brother who was also abused by their father and who happens to be dating the woman who witnessed the crime."

**Fine:** "The detective investigating happens to know her from years ago—they went to the same high school."

The difference:
- One coincidence is acceptable; it creates an interesting dynamic
- Stacked coincidences feel like the author is forcing connections
- Each coincidence should earn its keep—what does it add?

## Practical Test

When reviewing a scene or character, ask:

1. **How many significant elements are in play right now?** (More than 2-3 heavy elements in one scene = problem)

2. **Do these elements connect organically or through coincidence?** (One coincidence okay, multiple = contrived)

3. **Is there room to breathe?** (Can the audience process what's happening?)

4. **Does this serve the core movement or is it decoration?** (If decoration, lighten or cut it)

## The Car Wash Scene (Case Study)

In Breaking Bad, students discover Walter White working at a car wash and mock him, taking photos.

**What this scene does:**
- Advances the humiliation theme (light touch)
- Shows the gap between Walt's self-image and reality
- Builds pressure toward transformation
- Provides a moment that's painful but not devastating

**What this scene doesn't do:**
- Stack additional revelations (the student isn't also his drug dealer's nephew)
- Announce itself as symbolic
- Carry the entire weight of the theme
- Compete with heavier moments (like the diagnosis)

This is a light metaphor doing its job: supporting without overwhelming.

## For Tree Construction

1. **Tag every node with weight**: Heavy, Medium, Light
2. **Check density per scene**: No more than one heavy element per scene, usually
3. **Check coincidence load**: If two elements connect, that's fine; if five elements all happen to connect, rethink
4. **Ensure breathing room**: Heavy nodes need light nodes around them
5. **Review total distribution**: Story should have few heavy, several medium, many light
