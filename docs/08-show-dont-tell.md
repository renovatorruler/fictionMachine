# Show Don't Tell: The Metaphorical Stakes Problem

## The Core Violation

"Show don't tell" is not about prose style. It's about whether the story's **structure embodies its meaning** or merely **announces it**.

When we say Jerry's dating life is "a matter of life or death," we don't mean:
- A character says "This is life or death for Jerry!"
- The narration states "For Jerry, finding the right woman felt like survival itself"
- Jerry thinks "If I don't find someone, I'll die alone"

We mean: **The metaphorical elements of the story make it actually function as life or death.**

## What "Life or Death" Actually Means

Every story—even comedy—operates on life-or-death stakes at the metaphorical level. The question is whether those stakes are **embedded in the structure** or **asserted by the text**.

### The Seinfeld Example

Jerry can't date a woman his parents approve of. Why is this funny? Why does it work?

**Not because:** Someone says "Jerry's fear of commitment is really about his fear of becoming his parents."

**Because:** The show constructs situations where Jerry's romantic "survival" (his identity, his independence, his sense of self) is genuinely threatened by compatibility. The woman who looks different in different lighting isn't a quirk—it's Jerry's psyche manifesting: he literally cannot see a stable person because he cannot be stable himself.

The metaphor is structural. Jerry doesn't need to explain his fear. The situations embody it.

### What Makes Comedy Work

Comedy is tragedy that the protagonist survives (barely, ridiculously, by luck or absurdity). The stakes must be real at the metaphorical level:

- **George's employment struggles** aren't jokes about job hunting—they're about a man whose identity depends entirely on external validation, and who therefore cannot hold any position without destroying it
- **Elaine's relationships** aren't dating mishaps—they're a woman who wants intimacy but has contempt for the people who would provide it
- **Kramer's schemes** aren't wacky ideas—they're a man who has rejected conventional reality entirely, and the comedy is watching reality fail to constrain him

None of this is stated. It's shown through situation construction.

## The Structural Test

For any claimed stake, ask: **Is this stake embedded in the metaphorical structure, or is it asserted?**

### Asserted (Bad):

> "For Marcus, losing the competition would mean losing everything he'd worked for. It was, in a very real sense, life or death."

This tells us the stakes are high. We don't feel it because nothing in the structure makes it so.

### Embedded (Good):

Marcus is a chess prodigy from a coal mining town. His father died in the mines. His brother works in the mines. The competition scholarship is the only path out. If he loses, he goes back—not to poverty (that's too abstract), but to the specific mine where his father's body was never recovered, to work the same shaft, to breathe the same air.

The stakes aren't stated—they're constructed. The mine is death. The chessboard is life. Every move carries that weight without anyone saying it.

## How to Show Through Metaphor

### 1. Make the Concrete World Embody the Abstract Stakes

**Wrong:** "Elena felt trapped in her marriage."

**Right:** Elena's house has no windows facing the street. Her husband designed it that way. She realizes she hasn't seen the sidewalk from inside her own home in three years.

The house IS the marriage. The absence of windows IS the trap. No one needs to say "trapped."

### 2. Make Obstacles Literal Manifestations of Internal Barriers

**Wrong:** "Raj struggled to speak up for himself in meetings."

**Right:** Raj's office has a glass wall. Everyone can see him. When he starts to speak, he watches his reflection in the glass and loses his words. His mouth moves but nothing comes out. He sees himself failing to speak, and that vision prevents the speech.

The glass wall IS his self-consciousness. The reflection IS his internal observer. The silence manifests structurally.

### 3. Make Success and Failure Physically Embodied

**Wrong:** "If she didn't finish the project, her career would be over."

**Right:** She works on the 43rd floor. The elevator is broken. Every morning she climbs 43 flights. Her knees are giving out. She's 58. The project deadline is in two weeks. After that, they're moving the department to a building with working elevators—but she won't be invited.

The climb IS her career. The failing knees ARE her fading relevance. The new building IS the future that doesn't include her. The deadline is real because her body makes it real.

### 4. Make Character Relationships Embody Thematic Tensions

**Wrong:** "The father and son had a complicated relationship marked by unspoken expectations."

**Right:** The father taught the son to fish when he was six. They haven't gone fishing in twenty years. The father still keeps the son's child-sized rod in the garage, oiled and ready. The son sees it every time he visits. Neither mentions it. The son is forty-two and has never taken his own children fishing.

The fishing rod IS the relationship. Its presence IS the unspoken expectation. The son's failure to fish with his own children IS the cycle continuing. Nothing needs to be explained.

## Comedy Specifically

In comedy, the "life or death" stakes manifest as:

### Identity Death
- Jerry can't commit → If he commits, the Jerry who values freedom dies
- The commitment-capable Jerry is a different person
- Every relationship is an existential threat to his self-concept

### Social Death
- George needs people to think he's successful → If they see through him, he ceases to exist
- His entire being is the performance of competence
- Every exposure is annihilation

### Reality Death
- Kramer operates outside consensus reality → If forced to conform, his world ends
- The comedy is watching normal physics fail to apply to him
- Every brush with convention threatens to make him ordinary

These stakes are never stated. They're constructed through situation after situation that embodies them.

## The Metaphor Engine Application

When building the metaphor tree:

### At Abstract Level
Don't write: "The protagonist faces high stakes."
Write: Define what death looks like for this protagonist. What ends if they fail? Their identity? Their world? Their possibility of meaning?

### At Concrete Level
Don't write: "The setting reflects her internal state."
Write: Make the setting BE her internal state. The house with no windows. The mine shaft. The 43-flight climb. The fishing rod in the garage.

### At Structure Level
Don't write: "This scene shows how trapped she feels."
Write: Construct a scene where the trap is literal and physical, and let the audience make the connection.

### At Scene Level
Don't write: "He looked at her, knowing everything would change."
Write: He looked at her. She was wearing his mother's earrings. She didn't know. He had given them to her last week, told her he bought them at an antique store.

## The Rule

**If you can state the meaning in a sentence, you haven't shown it.**

If your story requires the sentence "This represents his fear of failure" to be understood, you haven't embedded the fear in the structure.

The metaphor tree should make it impossible NOT to feel the stakes—not because anyone says they're high, but because the world itself is constructed to embody them.

## Checklist

For every major element, ask:

1. **Can I state what this "means" in a sentence?** If yes, it's probably told, not shown.

2. **Would someone feel the stakes without any internal monologue or dialogue explaining them?** If no, rebuild the structure.

3. **Is the concrete world doing the work, or is the text doing the work?** The world should do the work.

4. **Could I remove all dialogue and narration and still have the meaning come through in the situation?** This is the test.

5. **Is the "life or death" literal at the metaphorical level?** Not asserted—constructed.

If any element fails these tests, the metaphor tree needs reconstruction, not cosmetic revision.
