# Anti-Patterns: What Not To Do

This document catalogs common mistakes in AI-generated storytelling. Each anti-pattern includes examples of what's wrong and how to fix it.

---

## 1. The Lighthouse Problem

**What it is:** Reaching for obvious, decorative symbols that announce themselves as meaningful.

**Bad:**
> "Elena clutched the lighthouse pendant her grandmother gave her. In the storm of her life, it was her beacon of hope."

> "The old cookbook, passed down through four generations, represented the family's unbroken bond."

> "As Marcus planted the oak sapling, he knew it symbolized the new beginnings he sought."

**Why it's wrong:**
- The symbol announces itself ("beacon of hope," "represented," "symbolized")
- It's decorative—remove it and the story doesn't change
- It's a cliché (lighthouses, cookbooks, trees = hope, family, growth)
- The audience is told what to feel instead of experiencing it

**Better:**
> Elena's hand went to her throat, to the small weight that hung there. Her grandmother had worn it for sixty years. It meant nothing. It meant everything. She didn't know which.

Or better yet—don't have a pendant. Let the meaning live in action and choice, not objects.

## 2. The Trauma Stack

**What it is:** Loading a single character with excessive backstory conditions to create "depth."

**Bad:**
> "Maya was a Syrian refugee who lost her parents in the war, now raising her disabled younger brother alone while battling depression, working three jobs, and hiding her undocumented status from her abusive landlord."

**Why it's wrong:**
- Each element could be meaningful; together they collapse into noise
- The character becomes a collection of conditions, not a person
- No single element gets room to breathe or develop
- It's trauma tourism—stacking suffering for emotional manipulation

**Better:**
Pick one or two elements and let them resonate:
> Maya's brother couldn't walk. She'd carried him across two borders. Some days she still felt his weight on her back, even when he was sitting right there.

## 3. The Coincidence Cascade

**What it is:** Connecting characters and events through implausible coincidences.

**Bad:**
> "The detective investigating her husband's murder turned out to be her estranged brother, who was dating the witness, who happened to work at the same hospital where their mother died, which was owned by the company her husband was about to expose."

**Why it's wrong:**
- One coincidence is acceptable; five is contrived
- It makes the story feel constructed rather than lived
- It shortcuts the work of building genuine connections
- The audience stops believing

**Better:**
One coincidence, used deliberately:
> The detective knew her face. They'd gone to the same high school. He'd asked her to prom once; she'd said no. Twenty years later, here they were.

That's enough. Let it create tension. Don't stack more.

## 4. The Both-Sides Ending

**What it is:** Resolving conflict through "mutual understanding" without anyone actually changing.

**Bad:**
> "In the end, both the corporation and the activists realized they had more in common than they thought. By listening to each other, they found a middle ground that honored everyone's concerns."

> "Sarah finally understood that her mother had only been trying to protect her, and her mother saw that Sarah needed room to grow. They hugged and agreed to communicate better."

**Why it's wrong:**
- It's false resolution—nothing actually changed
- It implies all conflicts are misunderstandings (they're not)
- It avoids taking a position
- "Mutual understanding" is often code for "the oppressed party accepted their oppression"

**Better:**
> The corporation won. They always did. But Elena published the documents anyway. Three journalists picked up the story. One got fired. One got a Pulitzer nomination. The corporation settled out of court, admitted nothing, and opened a new factory in Vietnam. Elena called it a victory. She was lying, and she knew it.

The conflict isn't resolved—it's survived. The story takes a position.

---

## 5. The Passive Antagonist

**What it is:** Creating villains who are explained, excused, or sympathized into harmlessness.

**Bad:**
> "The CEO wasn't evil—he was just a product of a system that rewarded ruthlessness. Deep down, he wanted to do the right thing, but the shareholders wouldn't let him. His own childhood trauma made it hard for him to connect with others."

**Why it's wrong:**
- It excuses the antagonist's actions before they've even acted
- It diffuses tension by making the villain a victim
- It prevents the audience from feeling anything clearly
- "Not evil, just complicated" often means "we're afraid to commit"

**Better:**
> The CEO believed he was saving the company. He was wrong, but he believed it. That's what made him dangerous—you can't negotiate with a man who thinks he's the hero.

Show his belief without excusing his actions. Let the audience judge.

## 6. The Vague Consequence

**What it is:** Gesturing at costs without naming them.

**Bad:**
> "The war took its toll on everyone."
> "There would be challenges ahead."
> "The cost was high, but she knew it was worth it."
> "Things would never be the same."

**Why it's wrong:**
- "Toll," "challenges," "cost," "things"—these are placeholders, not consequences
- The audience can't feel what isn't specific
- It lets the writer avoid committing to actual stakes
- It's lazy—it sounds meaningful while meaning nothing

**Better:**
> The war killed her father and both uncles. Her mother stopped speaking for two years.

> The cost: forty-three dead, including six children. Two villages burned. Grain prices tripled. Her nephew asked if it was her fault. She said yes.

> Things would never be the same: specifically, she would never sleep through the night again, and her daughter would not speak to her for three years.

Name it. Bodies, years, limbs, relationships. Specific costs land; vague ones don't.

## 7. The Hollow Transformation

**What it is:** Characters who change because the plot requires it, not because the story earned it.

**Bad:**
> "After everything that happened, Marcus finally understood what really mattered. He quit his job, reconnected with his family, and learned to appreciate the simple things."

**Why it's wrong:**
- "Finally understood" is a cheat—what specifically shifted?
- The transformation is stated, not shown
- "What really mattered" and "simple things" are clichés
- There's no ordeal—it just happened

**Better:**
> Marcus didn't quit his job. He got fired. He spent two months applying for positions he was overqualified for. His wife picked up extra shifts. One night, his daughter asked him to help with math homework, and he realized he didn't know what grade she was in. He learned. Slowly. It took three years before he stopped introducing himself with his old job title.

Transformation is slow, specific, and costs something. It's not a montage.

## 8. The Universal Empathy Trap

**What it is:** Making sure the audience understands everyone's perspective so thoroughly that no one is wrong.

**Bad:**
> "From the soldiers' perspective, they were just following orders. The refugees saw them as monsters, but both sides were victims of a system larger than themselves. There were no villains here—only people trapped by circumstance."

**Why it's wrong:**
- "No villains" often means "no accountability"
- Understanding someone's perspective doesn't excuse their actions
- It prevents the story from taking a moral position
- It's a cop-out disguised as sophistication

**Better:**
> The soldiers were following orders. They were also burning houses with people inside. Both of these things were true. History would not absolve them just because they were scared.

You can understand and still judge. Empathy is not absolution.

---

## 9. The Neat Ending

**What it is:** Tying up all threads so nothing remains unresolved.

**Bad:**
> "As the sun set on their wedding day, all the old hurts were forgotten. Her father walked her down the aisle, finally at peace with her choices. Her ex sent a gracious note. Even the weather cooperated."

**Why it's wrong:**
- Real life doesn't tie up neatly—stories that do feel false
- "Finally at peace" is almost always unearned
- It denies the complexity of actual human relationships
- The weather cooperating is literally pathetic fallacy

**Better:**
> Her father walked her down the aisle. He'd said he would, and he did. He didn't mention his objections; she didn't mention that she'd heard him on the phone with her aunt, calling it a mistake. They would never discuss it. This was love, in their family.

Leave threads dangling. Let discomfort remain. The audience will supply their own resolution—or learn to sit with ambiguity.

## 10. The Mechanical Echo

**What it is:** Forcing thematic parallels so hard they become obvious.

**Bad:**
> In the opening scene, young Thomas builds a sandcastle and watches the waves destroy it. In the final scene, old Thomas stands at the same beach. "Some things," he says, looking at the waves, "you just can't hold onto." He smiles, at peace.

**Why it's wrong:**
- The parallel is too neat, too announced
- The character literally states the theme
- The smile undercuts any genuine emotion
- It's technically an echo but feels manufactured

**Better:**
> Thomas's mother took him to this beach when he was six. They built something. He doesn't remember what—a castle, probably. He remembers her hands, sandy and warm. She's been dead for forty years. He doesn't come here to remember. He comes here because his body remembers, and sometimes that's enough.

The echo is present but buried. The theme isn't stated. The ending isn't resolved with a smile.

## 11. The Dialogue Thesis Statement

**What it is:** Having characters explicitly state the theme or their arc.

**Bad:**
> "You know what I've learned, after all this?" Maria said, looking at the sunset. "That family isn't about blood. It's about who shows up."

> "I used to think strength meant never asking for help," he said. "Now I know real strength is admitting when you can't do it alone."

**Why it's wrong:**
- Characters don't talk like this
- It insults the audience's intelligence
- The theme should emerge from action, not announcement
- It's the story congratulating itself

**Better:**
> Maria didn't say anything when her sister arrived. They hadn't spoken in four years. There was nothing to say that would be true. Her sister picked up a box and started carrying it to the truck. Maria picked up another.

The theme is there. It's in the action. No one needs to announce it.

---

## 12. The Hedged Position

**What it is:** Refusing to commit to what the story believes.

**Bad:**
> "Was she right to leave? There was no easy answer. Both choices had merit. She would never know for certain if she'd made the right decision, but she'd made a decision, and that was something."

**Why it's wrong:**
- "No easy answer" and "both choices had merit" are non-commitments
- "That was something" is meaningless filler
- The story is afraid to have an opinion
- The audience wanted a story, not a philosophy seminar

**Better:**
> She left. It was the right thing to do. It was also a betrayal. Both of these were true and neither canceled the other. She would carry it. That's what the right thing costs sometimes.

The story commits: it was right. But it also names the cost. This is not "both sides"—it's complexity with a spine.

---

## Using This Document

When reviewing any element of a metaphor tree—abstract, concrete, structure, or scene—check against these anti-patterns:

1. Am I reaching for obvious symbols? (Lighthouse)
2. Am I stacking conditions for false depth? (Trauma Stack)
3. Am I connecting things through implausible coincidence? (Coincidence Cascade)
4. Am I resolving conflict through fake understanding? (Both-Sides)
5. Am I excusing my antagonist into harmlessness? (Passive Antagonist)
6. Am I gesturing at costs without naming them? (Vague Consequence)
7. Am I stating transformation instead of earning it? (Hollow Transformation)
8. Am I understanding everyone into moral flatness? (Universal Empathy)
9. Am I tying up too neatly? (Neat Ending)
10. Am I forcing parallels too hard? (Mechanical Echo)
11. Am I having characters state the theme? (Dialogue Thesis)
12. Am I refusing to commit? (Hedged Position)

If yes to any of these—rewrite before proceeding.
