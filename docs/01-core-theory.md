# Core Theory: Everything is a Metaphor

## The Common Misconception

When people hear "metaphor," they think of:
- A lighthouse representing hope
- A tapestry symbolizing family bonds
- A multi-generational cookbook as heritage
- A storm mirroring inner turmoil

These are **decorative metaphors**—obvious, surface-level, and often the mark of amateur writing. LLMs are particularly prone to this, reaching for flowery visual elements that announce "I am symbolic!"

## The Correct Understanding

**Every element in a story is a metaphor.**

Not just the obvious symbolic ones. Everything:
- Walter White being a high school chemistry teacher
- The beige walls of his house
- The Pontiac Aztek he drives
- His chemistry lecture about change in the pilot

The lecture about chemistry being "the study of change" isn't decorative symbolism—it's structural. It echoes the entire series. This is what separates good writing from mediocre writing.

## The Principle

If an element is in the story, it carries meaning. There is nothing superfluous in good storytelling. Elements connect based on natural relationships, but those connection points are where metaphors touch.

### Bad Metaphor Usage
> "She clutched the lighthouse pendant her grandmother gave her, a beacon of hope in her darkest hour."

This announces itself. It's decorative. It could be removed without affecting the story's structure.

### Good Metaphor Usage
> A high school chemistry teacher explains to bored students that chemistry is about transformation—one thing becoming another, matter changing states, nothing ever truly destroyed.

This *is* the story. It doesn't announce itself as metaphor. It functions as a normal scene while resonating with everything that follows.

## Implications for Story Generation

When building a metaphor tree:

1. **Don't hunt for places to insert symbolism.** Instead, ensure every element you choose already carries the thematic weight naturally.

2. **Mundane choices are metaphors too.** The character's job, car, neighborhood, breakfast routine—all of these either reinforce or undermine your core movement.

3. **Test by removal.** If you can remove an element without affecting the story's thematic resonance, it's either decorative (bad) or disconnected (worse).

4. **Echo, don't announce.** The best metaphors function as normal story elements while quietly resonating with the whole.

## The Tree Structure

The metaphor tree isn't a checklist of symbols to insert. It's a framework ensuring coherence:

- **Root**: Core emotional/thematic movement
- **Branches**: Each major story element, chosen because it naturally embodies some aspect of the root
- **Leaves**: Specific details, all of which should either reinforce or meaningfully contrast with their parent branches

A breadth-first traversal of a well-constructed tree produces a story where everything matters—not because we artificially loaded it with meaning, but because we chose elements that inherently carry it.
