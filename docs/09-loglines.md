# Loglines: The Exception in the Loop

## What a Logline Is

A logline is a one-sentence summary that captures the core concept of a story. It's not a plot summary—it's a **hook**.

Think of it this way: Your protagonist's life is a forever-running loop. The logline describes the **exception** that breaks that loop.

> A high school chemistry teacher discovers he has terminal cancer. He decides to cook meth to secure his family's future.

The loop: Walter White teaches chemistry, comes home, lives a quiet life.
The exception: Terminal cancer + the decision to cook meth.

That's the logline. Everything else is story.

## The Exception Model

Every character exists in a loop—their ordinary world, their routine, their equilibrium. A logline captures:

1. **Who they are** (the loop they're in)
2. **What breaks the loop** (the exception)
3. **What they do about it** (the action)

### Loop → Exception → Action

**Jaws:**
> Loop: A police chief runs a small beach town.
> Exception: A killer shark arrives during tourist season.
> Action: He must hunt it down despite the mayor's opposition.

**The Godfather:**
> Loop: A war hero distances himself from his crime family.
> Exception: His father is nearly assassinated.
> Action: He must become the new Don to protect his family.

**Alien:**
> Loop: A commercial space crew is in cryosleep, heading home.
> Exception: They're awakened to investigate a distress signal.
> Action: They must survive when the rescue becomes a hunt.

## The Formula

A working logline contains:

```
When [INCITING INCIDENT], [PROTAGONIST TYPE] must [ACTION/GOAL] before/against [STAKES/ANTAGONIST].
```

Or more simply:

```
[CHARACTER] + [EXCEPTION] + [ACTION] + [STAKES]
```

### Length
- 25-50 words maximum
- One sentence (two at most for complex stories)
- If you can't say it in one breath, it's too long

## What Makes a Logline Work

### 1. Irony

The best loglines contain inherent irony—a contradiction that makes the premise compelling.

**Good irony:**
> A toon-hating detective must rely on a cartoon rabbit to solve a murder.

> A germaphobic man must share an apartment with a slob to afford rent.

> A computer hacker discovers that reality itself is a computer program.

The irony creates immediate tension. You understand the conflict before you know the plot.

### 2. Specificity Without Detail

Use **character types**, not names. "A retired cop" tells us more than "John McClane."

**Bad:** "Sarah must save her city."
**Good:** "A disgraced trauma surgeon must perform an impossible operation to stop a bomber."

The specificity (disgraced, trauma surgeon, impossible operation, bomber) creates a vivid picture. The lack of names keeps it universal.

### 3. Active Verbs

Loglines need action, not contemplation.

**Passive verbs to avoid:**
- learns
- discovers (unless paired with action)
- realizes
- comes to understand
- struggles with

**Active verbs to use:**
- must stop
- fights to
- races against
- hunts
- escapes
- infiltrates
- protects

**Bad:** "A woman learns to accept herself after a difficult divorce."
**Good:** "A woman infiltrates her ex-husband's wedding to steal back her grandmother's ring."

### 4. Clear Stakes

What happens if they fail? The stakes should be implied or stated.

**Vague stakes:** "...or face the consequences."
**Clear stakes:** "...before the bomb destroys Manhattan."

**Vague:** "A detective investigates a crime."
**Clear:** "A detective has 48 hours to find a kidnapped girl before she's sold."

### 5. The Antagonist or Obstacle

The conflict should be apparent. If your logline has no obstacle, it's not a story.

**No obstacle:** "A chef opens a restaurant."
**With obstacle:** "A chef must open a restaurant in two weeks using only ingredients from dumpsters."

## What to Leave Out

### Don't include:
- Character names (use types)
- Subplot
- The ending
- Theme statements
- Multiple protagonists (unless it's essential)
- Backstory beyond what's necessary

### Don't write:
- "This is a story about..."
- "In a world where..."
- "...and learns what really matters"
- "...discovers the true meaning of..."

## Testing Your Logline

### The Bar Test
Can you say it to a stranger at a bar and have them immediately want to know more? If you need to explain or qualify, it's not working.

### The Irony Test
Is there inherent conflict in the premise itself? Does the combination of character and situation create friction?

### The Action Test
Is there a clear action the protagonist must take? Or are they just experiencing things?

### The Stakes Test
What happens if they fail? Is it clear?

### The Loop/Exception Test
Can you identify the normal loop and the exception that breaks it?

## Examples Analyzed

### Breaking Bad
> A terminally ill high school chemistry teacher turns to manufacturing meth to secure his family's future.

- **Loop:** High school chemistry teacher (mundane, respectable)
- **Exception:** Terminal cancer (breaks the future he planned)
- **Action:** Turns to manufacturing meth (drastic, criminal)
- **Irony:** Teacher becomes criminal; chemistry used for drugs
- **Stakes:** Implied—family's future, his mortality

### The Shawshank Redemption
> A banker sentenced to life in prison for a murder he didn't commit uses his financial skills to gain favor with guards while plotting his escape.

- **Loop:** Banker (respectable, orderly life)
- **Exception:** Wrongful conviction
- **Action:** Uses skills to plot escape
- **Irony:** Banking skills useful in prison
- **Stakes:** Freedom or life in prison

### Get Out
> A young Black man visits his white girlfriend's family estate, only to discover a sinister plot targeting Black people.

- **Loop:** Normal relationship, meeting the parents
- **Exception:** The sinister plot
- **Action:** Must survive/escape
- **Irony:** Meeting the parents becomes survival horror
- **Stakes:** His life, his body

### Juno
> A sharp-tongued teenage girl gets pregnant and decides to give the baby to a seemingly perfect suburban couple.

- **Loop:** Teenage life
- **Exception:** Pregnancy
- **Action:** Decides to give baby to couple
- **Irony:** "Seemingly perfect" signals the complication
- **Stakes:** The baby's future, her own choices

## For the Metaphor Engine

The logline is the **compressed core movement**. It should emerge naturally from your metaphor tree:

1. **Protagonist** = Your protagonist at concrete level
2. **Loop** = The ordinary world / trap mechanism
3. **Exception** = The catalyst
4. **Action** = The response to the difficult choice
5. **Stakes** = What's at risk

From the secession example tree:
> A respected council member who has spent 30 years advocating for patience must cast the deciding vote on her people's independence after her grandson is punished for speaking their language.

- **Loop:** 30 years of patience and accommodation
- **Exception:** Her grandson punished for speaking their language
- **Action:** Must cast deciding vote
- **Stakes:** Her people's independence (and the war that may follow)

## Write Many, Combine Best

Don't try to write the perfect logline in one attempt.

1. Write 10-20 versions
2. Identify what works in each
3. Combine the strongest elements
4. Test against the criteria above
5. Repeat until it passes all tests

The logline is often harder to write than the story itself—because it forces you to know exactly what your story is.
